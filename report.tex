\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% ============================================================

Infectious diseases spread through contact networks, and the structure of those
networks is not static: individuals respond to epidemic risk by avoiding
infectious contacts.
This \emph{adaptive} behaviour feeds back into transmission dynamics,
accelerating or arresting outbreaks in ways that a classical well-mixed SIR
model cannot capture \citep{gross2006}.
Understanding these coupled processes is central to epidemic forecasting and
the design of targeted interventions such as contact tracing and quarantine
policies.

\paragraph{Model.}
We study a stochastic SIR model on an evolving network of $N=200$ agents,
initially connected as an Erdős–Rényi random graph with edge probability
$p=0.05$ and 5 randomly chosen infected nodes.
At each discrete timestep, three synchronous operations update the state:
(i) each susceptible--infected (S--I) edge transmits infection with probability
$\beta$; (ii) each infected agent recovers with probability $\gamma$; and
(iii) each S--I edge is \emph{rewired} with probability $\rho$, whereby the
susceptible node severs the risky contact and reconnects to a random agent.
We place independent uniform priors on the three unknown parameters:
$\beta \sim \mathcal{U}(0.05, 0.50)$,
$\gamma \sim \mathcal{U}(0.02, 0.20)$,
$\rho \sim \mathcal{U}(0.0, 0.8)$,
with prior standard deviations
$\sigma_\beta^{\mathrm{prior}} = 0.130$,
$\sigma_\gamma^{\mathrm{prior}} = 0.052$,
$\sigma_\rho^{\mathrm{prior}} = 0.231$.

\paragraph{Data.}
The observed dataset comprises $R=40$ independent replicates, each produced
with the same fixed but unknown $(\beta,\gamma,\rho)$.
Each replicate provides three observables: the time series of infected fraction,
the time series of rewiring counts, and the final degree distribution of the
network (Figure~\ref{fig:obs_data}).
The underlying contact network trajectory is never observed.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{obs_data}
  \caption{Observed data across 40 replicates: infection fraction (left),
           rewiring events (centre), final degree distribution (right).}
  \label{fig:obs_data}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Likelihood intractability.}
Evaluating the likelihood $p(\text{data}\mid\beta,\gamma,\rho)$ exactly requires
summing over all possible trajectories of the latent contact network, which grows
combinatorially with $N$ and time.
Even a single trajectory evaluation is prohibitively expensive because the
rewiring step introduces a complex dependency between the infection state and
the network topology at every timestep.
Classical maximum-likelihood or MCMC methods that require pointwise likelihood
evaluations are therefore infeasible.

\paragraph{Approach.}
\emph{Simulation-based inference} (SBI) circumvents this by replacing the
likelihood with forward simulation: given any proposed $(\beta,\gamma,\rho)$,
one can cheaply generate synthetic datasets and compare them to the observations.
We apply a hierarchy of SBI methods of increasing sophistication, beginning with
rejection Approximate Bayesian Computation (ABC;
Section~\ref{sec:rabc}) and progressing through semi-automatic summary
construction \citep{fearnhead2012}, LASSO-based summary selection
\citep{blum2013}, regression adjustment \citep{beaumont2002}, synthetic
likelihood MCMC \citep{wood2010}, ABC-MCMC \citep{marjoram2003}, and Sequential
Monte Carlo ABC \citep{sisson2007} (Section~\ref{sec:advanced}).
A cross-method comparison and discussion of identifiability close the report
(Sections~\ref{sec:comparison} and~\ref{sec:conclusion}).

\section{Summary Statistics}
\label{sec:summaries}
% ============================================================

Because acceptance probability in ABC falls exponentially with summary
dimension \citep{blum2013}, we compress the raw time series to a small
set of scalar features.
A naive approach is to concatenate all available scalars into one vector.
The problem is that in a standardised Euclidean distance, a modality with
more features contributes proportionally more to the total distance —
a simulation can be accepted purely because its infection curve is close,
even if its rewiring and degree statistics are far off.
We therefore design a \emph{balanced} set of 15 features with deliberate
equal representation across the three modalities:
5 infection, 4 rewiring, and 6 degree summaries, all averaged across replicates.
Within each group, features are chosen for identifiability rather than
completeness (e.g.\ \texttt{epidemic\_duration} over \texttt{final\_inf}
to better separate $\beta$ from $\rho$; cross-replicate standard deviations
to capture stochastic variability; degree quartiles and entropy over isolated/
high-degree fractions to describe the full distribution shape).

\subsection{Identifiability diagnostics}

Projecting the $M=10\,000$ prior-simulation summaries to 2D via PCA and
colouring by parameter value (Figure~\ref{fig:pca}) reveals clear gradients
for $\gamma$, moderate structure for $\beta$, and weak but non-trivial
structure for $\rho$.
This confirms the central identifiability challenge: $\beta$ drives spread
while $\rho$ suppresses it through rewiring, but their effects on the
aggregate infection curve partially cancel — high $\beta$ paired with high
$\rho$ can produce a similar epidemic trajectory to low $\beta$ paired with
low $\rho$. Infection-curve summaries alone therefore cannot fully separate
them; rewiring summaries carry the independent signal needed for both.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{pca_diagnostic}

           $\rho$ (left to right). Clear gradients for $\gamma$; partial
           confounding visible in $\beta$–$\rho$ panels.}
  \label{fig:pca}
\end{figure}

% ============================================================
\section{Rejection ABC}
\label{sec:rabc}
% ============================================================

We drew $M=10\,000$ prior samples, computed the standardised Euclidean distance
between each simulated and observed summary vector, and accepted draws below the
1\% empirical quantile \citep{pritchard1999}, giving $\varepsilon=1.2852$ and
$n_\mathrm{acc}=100$.
Standardised Euclidean distance is parameter-free and treats each $z$-scored summary
equally, avoiding manual weighting of modalities.
The 1\% quantile balances bias against variance: tighter tolerances reduce approximation
bias but increase Monte Carlo noise; the residual bias at $q=1\%$ motivates
regression adjustment (Section~\ref{sec:regadj}), which allows a looser 5\% tolerance.
Figure~\ref{fig:rabc_posteriors} shows the marginal posteriors; Table~\ref{tab:rabc}
gives the numerical summaries.

           \emph{balanced} set, $\varepsilon=1.2852$, $n_\mathrm{acc}=100$.}
  \label{fig:rabc_posteriors}
\end{figure}

$\gamma$ is best-identified (directly controls epidemic duration),

the PCA diagnostic and the positive $(\beta,\rho)$ posterior correlation:
high $\beta$ can be offset by high $\rho$ to produce a similar epidemic,
so the posterior trades off transmission rate against rewiring rate.

\section{Advanced Methods}
\label{sec:advanced}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Semi-Automatic ABC \citep{fearnhead2012}}
\label{sec:semiauto}

\citet{fearnhead2012} show that the optimal ABC summaries are the
posterior means $\mathbb{E}[\theta\mid x]$, which can be approximated by
fitting Ridge regression from the balanced features to each parameter on
the pilot draws.
In-sample $R^2$ values confirm strong predictability:
$R^2_\beta=0.812$, $R^2_\gamma=0.857$, $R^2_\rho=0.979$.
Running rejection ABC in the resulting 3-dimensional projected space
($\varepsilon=0.520$, $n_\mathrm{acc}=100$) substantially tightens the
posterior for $\beta$ and provides moderate gains for $\gamma$ and $\rho$
(Figure~\ref{fig:semiauto}, Table~\ref{tab:contraction}).

\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{semiauto_marginals}
  \caption{Balanced rejection ABC (blue) vs.\ semi-automatic ABC (orange),
           $q=1\%$.}
  \label{fig:semiauto}
\end{figure}

\subsection{LASSO-Based Summary Selection \citep{blum2013}}
\label{sec:lasso}

Fitting LassoCV from summaries to each parameter on the pilot simulations
retained 14/15 features for $\beta$ ($\alpha=6\times10^{-4}$) and all 15 for
$\gamma$ and $\rho$ ($\alpha=8\times10^{-4}$).
No feature is entirely redundant, so the LASSO-selected set is
effectively identical to the balanced set and yields the same posterior
(Table~\ref{tab:contraction}).

\subsection{Regression Adjustment \citep{beaumont2002}}
\label{sec:regadj}

After accepting draws at a loose tolerance ($q=5\%$, $\varepsilon=1.706$,
$n_\mathrm{acc}=500$), each accepted $\theta^{(i)}$ is corrected for the
residual gap between its simulated summary $s^{(i)}$ and $s_\mathrm{obs}$:
\[
  \tilde{\theta}^{(i)}
    = \theta^{(i)} - \hat{B}^\top (s^{(i)} - s_\mathrm{obs}),
\]
where $\hat{B}$ is estimated by weighted least squares with Epanechnikov
kernel weights \citep{beaumont2002}.
Figure~\ref{fig:regadj_marginals} and Table~\ref{tab:regadj} show that
adjustment dramatically sharpens the posterior
($\sigma_\beta$: 0.099$\to$0.024, $\sigma_\gamma$: 0.033$\to$0.007,
$\sigma_\rho$: 0.087$\to$0.008).
Crucially, the adjusted posterior is nearly tolerance-invariant for
$q\lesssim10\%$, whereas the raw posterior degrades rapidly at loose tolerances.

\subsection{Synthetic Likelihood \citep{wood2010}}
\label{sec:sl}

At each MCMC proposal $\theta$, we simulate $N_\mathrm{sim}=150$ datasets
($R_\mathrm{sim}=40$ replicates each), giving an $N_\mathrm{sim}/p$ ratio of
$10.0$ for our $p=15$ summary features, and approximate the intractable
likelihood as a multivariate Gaussian fitted to the $N_\mathrm{sim}$ summary
vectors, using Ledoit--Wolf shrinkage for the covariance
\citep{ledoitwolf2004}:
\[
  \log\hat{p}_\mathrm{SL}(s_\mathrm{obs}\mid\theta)
  = \log\mathcal{N}(s_\mathrm{obs};\,\hat{\mu}(\theta),\,\hat{\Sigma}(\theta)).
\]
This is embedded in a Metropolis--Hastings MCMC with an
\textbf{Adaptive Metropolis} (AM) proposal \citep{haario2001}.
Rather than fixing step sizes in advance, AM updates the proposal
covariance online after an initial warm-up of $t_0=100$ steps:
\[
  \Sigma_\mathrm{prop}(t) = \frac{2.38^2}{d}\,
  \mathrm{Cov}(\theta_1,\ldots,\theta_t) + \epsilon I,
  \quad d=3,
\]
so the chain learns the posterior shape automatically and no manual
step-size tuning is required \citep{roberts1997}.
Configuration: $N_\mathrm{steps}=2\,000$, burn-in $=500$,
initialised at the regression-adjusted posterior mean
$(0.143,\, 0.079,\, 0.299)$.
The chain achieved an acceptance rate of $24.0\%$, consistent with the
optimal AM target \citep{roberts1997}.

\subsection{ABC-MCMC \citep{marjoram2003}}
\label{sec:abcmcmc}

At each step, we accept a proposal $\theta^* = \theta_t + \eta$, $\eta \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \Sigma)$ if $\rho(S(x^*), S(x_\text{obs})) \leq \varepsilon$, with $R = 40$ replicates per
proposal and $\varepsilon$ imported from rejection ABC at the $1\%$ quantile. We compare MCMC across the three summary
variants: \textbf{balanced}, \textbf{LASSO-selected}, and \textbf{semi-automatic} (with $\varepsilon$ recalibrated in the projected space).

We tune the proposal $\Sigma$ in three stages — original, adaptive $1\times$, and adaptive
$0.5\times$ \citep{roberts2009}, with the final selection being the $0.5\times$ as it restores target
acceptance rates while achieving $\hat{R} < 1.001$ for all parameters. All results
use $N = 30{,}000$ steps with burn-in $b = 1{,}000$.

The semi-automatic variant gives the tightest posteriors for $\beta$ ($\text{rel} = 0.22$) and
$\rho$ ($\text{rel} = 0.28$), but at the cost of $\gamma$ precision ($\text{rel} = 0.72$ vs
$0.39$--$0.40$ for balanced and LASSO). This is a consequence of the ridge projection optimising
jointly across parameters. As expected, balanced and LASSO perform near-identically. The $\beta$--$\rho$
correlation persists across all variants. Fixed $\varepsilon$ MCMC cannot resolve
this confounding.

\subsection{SMC-ABC \citep{sisson2007}}
\label{sec:smcabc}

ABC-MCMC's fixed $\varepsilon$ limits posterior accuracy and its serially correlated samples
reduce ESS. We refine $N = 100$ particles through a decreasing
tolerance sequence $\varepsilon_t = Q_{0.75}(\{\rho_i^{(t)}\})$ \citep{beaumont2009},
producing asymptotically independent samples and perturb particles via $\mathcal{N}(0, \Sigma)$ from the ABC-MCMC,
run for $T = 15$ generations. A refined run
with $\Sigma$ halved consistently outperforms the original.

SMC substantially improves on ABC-MCMC across all variants: $\beta$ shrinkage
improves from $0.22$ to $0.06$ and $\rho$ from $0.28$ to $0.08$ for the semi-automatic
variant, and the $\beta$--$\rho$ correlation is substantially reduced.
The $\gamma$ penalty from the ridge projection is also smaller than under MCMC
($0.18$ vs $0.72$), suggesting progressive $\varepsilon$ tightening partially recovers
lost $\gamma$ information (see Table~\ref{tab:compare} for full numerical results).

  \centering

  \caption{Joint posterior $\beta$ vs $\rho$ for SMC-ABC (refined $0.5\times$ run, final
           generation) across all three summary variants. The $\beta$--$\rho$ correlation
           visible under ABC-MCMC is substantially reduced, with particles concentrating

($\hat\beta\approx0.14$–$0.15$, $\hat\gamma\approx0.079$–$0.081$,

large accepted set ($n=500$), tolerance-robust, and verifiable via

combined with a multivariate normal approximation that may be
overconfident for non-Gaussian summary statistics.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% ============================================================

We have applied a progressive hierarchy of simulation-based inference methods
to recover the three parameters $(\beta, \gamma, \rho)$ of an adaptive-network
SIR model from $R=40$ replicate observations.

\paragraph{Identifiability.}
The clearest finding is that the three parameters are not equally identifiable
from aggregate data.
$\gamma$ is the best-constrained: it directly controls epidemic duration and
is strongly encoded in the infection time series, allowing even rejection ABC
to shrink its posterior to 38\% of the prior width.
$\beta$ and $\rho$ are harder to separate because both modulate epidemic size
through different mechanisms — higher transmission and lower rewiring produce
similar infection curves — leaving a genuine positive posterior correlation
between them that persists across all methods and summary sets.
Rewiring summaries carry the independent signal needed to partially resolve
this confounding; infection-curve summaries alone are essentially uninformative
about $\rho$, and this structure is intrinsic to the observables rather than a sampler artefact.

\paragraph{Method comparison.}
Each method addresses a specific limitation of its predecessor.
Semi-automatic ABC reduces summary dimensionality via ridge projection, tightening
$\beta$ and $\rho$ at the cost of $\gamma$ precision.
Regression adjustment \citep{beaumont2002} dramatically sharpens all posteriors
($\sigma_\rho$: 0.087$\to$0.008) and is robust to the choice of tolerance for $q\lesssim10\%$.
ABC-MCMC \citep{marjoram2003} achieves well-converged chains ($\hat{R}<1.001$) but its fixed
$\varepsilon$ leaves the $\beta$--$\rho$ confounding unresolved.
SMC-ABC \citep{sisson2007} delivers the best overall performance, with the semi-automatic
variant reaching shrinkage of 0.06 for $\beta$ and 0.08 for $\rho$.
Synthetic likelihood \citep{wood2010} yields the numerically sharpest posterior but its
low ESS (7.5--11.9) makes those standard deviations unreliable.

\paragraph{Limitations and future work.}
The most pressing limitation is the low ESS of the synthetic likelihood chain,
which could be addressed by longer runs, a better-conditioned covariance
estimator, or a more targeted initialisation strategy.
For SMC-ABC, increasing the particle count beyond $N=100$ would reduce Monte
Carlo noise in the final generation and give more stable shrinkage estimates.
More broadly, the $\beta$--$\rho$ confounding is ultimately a limitation of
the aggregate summary statistics: individual-level contact tracing data, if
available, would provide direct information about rewiring events and likely
resolve the degeneracy.
Finally, neural simulation-based inference methods \citep[e.g.][]{cranmer2020}
that learn flexible approximations to the posterior directly from simulations
represent a natural next step, bypassing the need for hand-crafted summary
statistics altogether.
